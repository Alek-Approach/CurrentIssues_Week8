Adapted for B2+ from: “Spending growth slowed at retail stores in October as high prices give shoppers pause” — The Associated Press, November 2026 Reading time ≈ 10 min

Shoppers slow down before the holidays

1American shoppers increased their spending more slowly in October. According to the Commerce Department, retail sales rose just 0.2% that month, down from a 0.8% gain in September. With prices still high and the economy uncertain, many people pulled back on cars, clothes, furniture and even their hobbies just before the holiday shopping season.

2Two sectors had a big effect on the total. Sales at gas stations rose 1.5% after jumping 30.3% in September; without gas stations, retail sales grew by only 0.1%. Sales at car and parts dealers fell 1.6%, reversing a 1.3% increase the month before. Without that sector, retail sales rose 0.6%.

3Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, argues that once higher prices are taken into account, consumer spending actually went down. The Consumer Price Index rose 0.4% in October, more than expected. In Long's view, “the cautious consumer is back”: budgets are tight, and middle- and moderate-income families are feeling the strain. She points out that falling gas prices in November could give households some much-needed cash, although a gallon of gas still costs nearly 20% more than a year ago.

4Business fell at clothing stores, furniture stores and even in the sporting goods, music, book and hobby sector. Long notes that this sector had been strong in the spring and summer, when shoppers focused on small, fun splurges. Online retailers were a bright spot, rising 1.2% thanks partly to a two-day Amazon Prime Day event in early October. Restaurants, the only service category in the report, rose 0.2%. The so-called “control group,” which leaves out food services, cars, building materials and gas and is used to calculate economic growth, rose a solid 0.6%.

5Prices remain the bigger story. Higher energy costs are spreading into airfares, public transportation and shipping, and grocery prices rose 0.4%, according to the Labor Department. The report also states that massive investment in artificial intelligence has made inflation worse by pushing up the price of computer chips, and therefore of laptops, phones and other electronics.

6Retailers are now preparing for Thanksgiving weekend, the unofficial start of holiday shopping, with heavily advertised deals for customers who increasingly buy only when they believe prices have dropped. Target says it is offering more than 8,000 new holiday gifts, most of them $20 or less, and Kohl's has added more gift sets.

7Many Americans continue to rely on credit cards, even though borrowing has become more expensive. Charles Wise of the credit reporting agency TransUnion notes that credit card balances reached $1.17 trillion, up 5.1% from a year earlier, but serious delinquency rates have held steady. In his view, this shows that consumers “remain disciplined borrowers heading into the holidays.”

This is an original classroom adaptation, not the full article. Short quotations are marked and attributed.

Who said what?

Individual → pairs10 min

Synthesis starts with knowing exactly where each idea comes from. Choose the source of each point.

1Retail sales rose 0.2% in October.

Official data: “according to the Commerce Department's report.”

2Budgets are tight for many households.

This is Long's interpretation of the data, not a number from the report.

3Consumers remain disciplined borrowers.

Wise draws this conclusion from TransUnion's credit data.

4Grocery prices increased 0.4%.

Prices data comes from the Labor Department.

5Investment in AI has made inflation worse.

No expert or data source is named for this claim. In your writing, you would attribute it to “the AP report,” and a careful reader might ask: what is the evidence?

6The hobby sector had been strong in spring and summer.

The article says “Long noted” this.

Adapted for B2+ from: “2026 holiday shopping trends: What consumers are planning” — Lea Grgich, National Retail Federation, October 28, 2026 · Survey press release: “Affordability shapes winter holiday shopping” Reading time ≈ 8 min

Deals, stores and experiences: holiday plans for 2026

1The National Retail Federation (NRF)'s October Holiday Consumer Survey, carried out by Prosper Insights & Analytics, found that 91% of consumers plan to celebrate the winter holidays this year. The NRF forecasts that holiday retail sales will grow 4.2% over 2025, reaching $1.013 trillion. According to the report, consumers are dealing with economic pressure but are still prioritizing their loved ones and traditions.

2Waiting for the deals. Almost two-thirds of shoppers (64%) say they are likely to wait until Thanksgiving weekend to do most of their holiday shopping, compared with 51% five years ago. Sales and discounts (56%) are the factor shoppers mention most when choosing where to shop, followed by quality (51%), selection (46%) and free shipping or shipping deals (37%).

3Online is cooling. Online is still the most popular place for holiday shopping, but only 53% of shoppers plan to buy gifts and holiday items online — the lowest share since 2015. People who are avoiding online shopping say they want to see items before buying (38%), be more thoughtful by shopping in person (23%) and enjoy the holiday atmosphere in stores (22%). Even online shoppers expect to do a smaller part of their shopping online: 52% this year, compared with 56% last year.

4Experiences reach a record. More than a quarter of shoppers (27%) plan to give an experience as a gift, up from 19% five years ago. Millennials remain the generation most likely to give experiences (38%, the same as before), but Gen Z pushed the category to a new high: it was the only generation in which more people plan to buy experience gifts this year. On wish lists, Gen Z (51%) and Millennials (50%) most want clothing and accessories, while Gen X (46%) and Boomers (59%) most want gift cards.

Original classroom adaptation of the NRF report. All figures are from the NRF survey.

Struggling or resilient?

Do they agree? Decide how the sources relate on each point, then check.

1Long and the NRF: shoppers are paying close attention to prices.

Long: budgets are tight. NRF: discounts are the top factor and 64% will wait for Thanksgiving deals. Same conclusion, different evidence.

2Long and the NRF: holiday spending will be healthy.

The NRF forecasts 4.2% growth. Long stresses strain on middle-income families but also says cheaper gas could free up cash — so there is some overlap.

3Wise and the NRF: consumers are coping well financially.

“Disciplined borrowers” and “resilient” shoppers point the same way.

4AP data and the NRF: online shopping is growing.

On the surface they disagree (online sales +1.2% vs online plans at their lowest since 2015). But they measure different things: one month's actual sales, boosted by Prime Day, vs shoppers' plans for the season. Good synthesis notices this.
